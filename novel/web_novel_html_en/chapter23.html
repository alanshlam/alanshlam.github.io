Chapter 23: Curtain Between Enemies

Secondary Characters

Captain Elias Marek (Meridian Federation)

Captain Elias Marek, a 32-year-old Federation officer from the industrial heartlands, had risen through the ranks not by blind ambition but by a quiet competence that masked his growing disillusionment. Lean and sharp-featured, with close-cropped dark hair prematurely threaded with gray and eyes that carried the weight of too many lost subordinates, Marek commanded respect through calm authority rather than bluster. A family man at heart—married with a young daughter waiting back home—he had never viewed the Combine as an existential enemy, seeing the border conflict instead as a pointless drain of blood and resources orchestrated by distant politicians. In the monastery kitchen, stripped of his weapons and confronted with the raw cost of war, Marek’s humanity surfaced fully: pragmatic yet compassionate, willing to trust an enemy officer’s word and order his men to save lives across the divide. His brief bond with Sean planted the seeds of doubt that would linger long after the guns resumed, a man who chose conscience over flag when it mattered most.

Historical Note: Humanity in the Midst of War

While the events in this chapter are fictional, they draw from real patterns in military history. Opposing forces have repeatedly set aside weapons for short, informal truces to recover and treat the wounded—often around neutral religious sites such as churches or monasteries, which were traditionally respected as sanctuaries.

Notable examples include local ceasefires during the Battle of Monte Cassino (1944), where German and Allied troops arranged brief pauses to evacuate casualties and, in some cases, traded medical supplies in the harsh Italian campaign. In the North African desert during WWII, the fighting earned the nickname “war without hate” due to acts of mutual medical aid and respect. Similar spontaneous humanitarian gestures occurred during the 1914 Christmas Truce on the Western Front, where soldiers from both sides recovered wounded, buried the dead together, and showed compassion across the lines.

These moments remind us that even in the brutality of war, basic human decency can surface when soldiers confront the shared cost of conflict. Such truces were almost always fragile and local—ordered by no high command, but born from exhaustion and recognition of a common humanity.

The sky had betrayed him. Minutes earlier, Sean had ruled the heavens in his fighter, engines roaring like a god’s defiance. Now he tumbled through smoke-choked air, parachute snapping open with bone-jarring force as Federation anti-air batteries turned the Sirona borderlands into a merciless meat grinder. Shot down and thrust into command of two shattered Combine companies, Sean led his exhausted, ammunition-starved survivors through days of desperate evasion—until a weathered stone monastery rose like a fragile miracle on the horizon. There, in a blood-slick kitchen divided by nothing more than a thin curtain, enemies laid down their weapons and discovered something more powerful than orders or flags: the stubborn, aching humanity still burning inside their souls. As he sat shoulder to shoulder with a Federation captain named Elias Marek, trading not bullets but bandages and broken truths, Sean wondered how many more boys would die before their leaders remembered they were human first.

From Heaven to Hell

The sky above Sirona had once felt like sanctuary. Up there, strapped into the cockpit, Sean had been untouchable—engines screaming, missiles streaking clean lines across the horizon. Two weeks of it. Two weeks of threading through Federation flak and living to tell the tale. But the borderlands chewed men up and spat out the pieces, and no altitude could save you forever.

Ground fire caught him at eight thousand feet.

The console lit up like a festival gone wrong—warnings blaring, hydraulics failing, the stick fighting him every inch. "Eject, eject," the automated voice droned, calm as a nun at prayer. Sean yanked the handle. The canopy blew. Cold air slammed him. Then the chute deployed with a vicious jerk that nearly snapped his spine.

He hit the dirt rolling, lungs burning, legs already numb from the impact. Smoke rose from the wreckage half a kilometer away. Federation patrols would be on it soon. No time for inventory. He grabbed his sidearm, the emergency med kit, and what ammunition he could salvage from the survival pack.

That was when he found the first Combine survivors—two ragged companies, or what was left of them. Maybe eighty men total, most limping, several on makeshift litters. Their captain had taken shrapnel to the throat two days earlier. The lieutenant died yesterday from infection. That left Sean, a damned aviator, as the senior officer still breathing.

"Ammo's down to three magazines per rifle," one sergeant reported, voice hoarse. "Wounded are slowing us. Federation armor swept the eastern ridge this morning."

Sean looked at the faces around him—hollow eyes, blood-crusted bandages, men who should have been rotated out weeks ago. He wasn't trained for this. Not really. But the alternative was dying in place.

"Strip the dead," he ordered, surprised at how steady his voice sounded. "Every round, every bandage. We move west at dusk, low ground, tree cover where we can find it. No heroics. We link up with the brigade or we don't make it."

The first night was a blur of whispers and suppressed groans. They dragged the worst wounded on ponchos, the fabric tearing against roots and rocks. Federation patrols swept nearby twice; each time Sean held his breath while his new command pressed into the mud, hearts hammering loud enough to give them away. One private—a kid who couldn't have been more than nineteen—started bleeding again from a gut wound. They packed it with dirt and prayed.

By the third day, luck had officially run dry. Sean's own leg throbbed from a graze he hadn't had time to treat properly. Fever flickered at the edges of his vision. The companies were down to forty-six effectives. The rest were either dead or left behind with a mercy round and a silent promise.

Then the monastery appeared on the horizon—stone walls weathered gray, a modest spire cutting the smoke-filled sky. A fragile white flag fluttered from the gate. Shelter. Medical supplies, maybe. Or just a place to die with a roof overhead.

"Approaching slow," Sean said. "Weapons ready, but don't look for a fight. If the Federation's already inside... we'll deal with it."

The men nodded. No one argued. They had nothing left to argue with.

Human nature still inside our souls

They reached the monastery as the light bled from the sky, the stone walls rising like a half-forgotten promise. The nuns met them at the gate—habits stained with old blood, faces carved with exhaustion that matched their own. No questions about sides or flags. Only one condition.

"Leave your weapons outside," the eldest sister said, voice quiet but iron-hard. "Both sides have men dying in there. This house belongs to God tonight, not to your war."

Sean hesitated only a moment. His leg burned with every step, and behind him forty-odd souls were breaking. They stacked their rifles and sidearms in a neat pile against the east wing wall, the metal clinking like bones. Across the courtyard, Federation soldiers did the same along the west wing. Two small mountains of death, separated by sacred ground.

Inside, the kitchen had become a slaughterhouse chapel.

Long wooden tables served as operating slabs. Lantern light flickered over open wounds, the air thick with the metallic reek of blood and the sharp bite of alcohol. A thin curtain—nothing more than faded linen—divided the room. On one side, Combine wounded. On the other, their enemies from just days ago.

A Federation voice cracked through the divide. "Bandages! For the love of God, does anyone have more bandages?"

Sean’s hand moved before his mind caught up. He pulled a sealed pack from his own dwindling kit and slid it under the curtain. It disappeared. A pause. Then a Federation hand shoved back a different packet.

"Plasma," a voice called, raw and desperate. "Type O if you’ve got it!"

Sean had one bag left. He passed it under. In return came clotting powder, then sutures, then morphine—the give and take frantic, like trading relics in the dark. Voices rose on both sides, no longer shouting orders but begging.

"Clotting agent—anything!"

"Sutures here!"

A Combine private screamed as a medic dug shrapnel from his thigh. On the other side of the curtain, a Federation boy answered with his own agonized moan.

Sean stared at the flimsy barrier. It was absurd. They had been killing each other across these fields, and now a scrap of cloth was all that kept the bullets from flying again. His hands trembled as he reached out and yanked the curtain aside.

The two sides stared at each other across the blood-slick floor.

For a heartbeat, everything froze. Rifles were gone, but hatred wasn’t. One Federation soldier gripped a scalpel like a weapon. A Combine sergeant tensed, ready to lunge.

"Help them," Sean said, voice cutting through the tension. It came out steadier than he felt. "Anyone who can still walk, get over there. Save who you can."

His medics hesitated, eyes wide. Then one moved. Then another. On the opposite side, a Federation captain—younger than Sean expected, face smeared with soot and fatigue—issued the same command.

"Assist them. Now."

The room shifted. Combine hands pressed gauze onto Federation wounds. Federation soldiers held down a thrashing Combine private while Sean’s own medic worked. Blood mixed on the floor, indistinguishable. A nun moved silently between them, handing out clean rags and whispered prayers that sounded like forgiveness no one had earned.

Sean caught the eye of the Federation captain across a table where a boy—no older than the one they’d lost yesterday—fought for every breath. For a moment, the war vanished. There was only the wet sound of sutures, the ragged breathing of the dying, and the terrible, stubborn refusal to let another soul slip away.

Human nature, buried under uniforms and flags and weeks of slaughter, clawed its way to the surface in that candlelit kitchen. Fragile. Ugly. And still alive.

To be human first

The last suture was tied. The last morphine dose administered. In the quiet that followed, the kitchen felt less like a battlefield and more like a tomb.

Sean slid down the stone wall until he sat on the cold floor, legs too heavy to hold him any longer. Blood soaked his sleeves—some his, most not. Beside him, the Federation captain, Elias Marek, did the same, shoulder to shoulder with the man he had been trying to kill only days earlier. Their uniforms, once symbols of opposing flags, were now indistinguishable under layers of dirt and dried blood.

For a long minute, neither spoke. The only sounds were the soft moans of the wounded and the distant, endless rumble of artillery that never truly stopped.

“How many?” Sean finally asked, voice scraped raw.

“Three,” Marek replied. He stared at his hands. “One didn’t make it. Slipped away while we were fighting for the others.” A pause, heavy as lead. “Yours?”

“Same.” Sean’s throat tightened. “Lost the youngest one an hour ago. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Didn’t even know his name until we checked the tags.” He let out a long, shuddering breath that carried the weight of every mile they had dragged those boys. “Sir… before I’m a soldier, I need to be human first. In this monastery, right now, I choose to be human. Not for any flag. Not for any cause.”

Marek closed his eyes. When he opened them, they shone with something between exhaustion and tears he refused to shed. “Thank you for saying it out loud. I trust you.” His voice cracked. “Here, in this place, I choose the same. Human first. This uniform… it feels like a lie tonight.” He gestured vaguely toward the bloodstained floor, the curtain now pulled aside like a surrendered banner. “I never believed the Combine was some existential threat. We could have shared the border. Traded. Lived. Instead, we’re feeding children to the machine.”

Sean felt the accusation rise in his chest—not at the man beside him, but at the faceless leaders safe in their bunkers and palaces. “If our commanders had even a fraction of your mind, how many graves would be empty right now? How many mothers would still have their sons?” His voice grew quieter, fiercer. “This war isn’t glory. It’s slaughter wearing a uniform. And we’re the ones paying the butcher’s bill.”

Marek gave a bitter, exhausted laugh. “If you ever run for president of the Combine, you’d have my vote. God help me.”

“Same,” Sean whispered. “Same.”

They sat in silence after that, two tired men sharing the same wall, the same grief, the same quiet fury at a universe that let this happen. Outside, the nuns moved like ghosts, offering water and prayers to both sides. No shots were fired. No orders given. Just the fragile, temporary victory of humanity over the machinery of hate.

Eventually, they rose. Sean gathered his remaining men. Marek did the same. They exchanged no salutes, only a single nod that carried more weight than any treaty. Sean led his battered companies toward the occupied village in the west, where a Combine brigade waited. The Federation men turned east, back toward their own lines.

As they marched under the indifferent stars, Sean’s mind drifted. In the haze of fatigue, he saw it clearly: a small house somewhere far from borders and flags. Ruby’s laughter filling the rooms. No more cockpits. No more blood on his hands. No more boys dying without names. He would end this war machine if it took every breath left in his body. For her. For the kids who never made it home. For the man he had sat beside on that kitchen floor.

The war would continue tomorrow. But tonight, in one small corner of hell, humanity had remembered its own face.
